The IdenTrust certificate, every step

From choosing the right IGC product to a verified PKCS12 keystore registered with the IRS — with the exact OpenSSL commands, what each file is, and how to check your work at every step. Grounded in the certificate chain already checked into the MeF repo.

TL;DR — the whole certificate process in two sentences

You generate a private key that never leaves your server, send IdenTrust a signing request (the public half plus your identity), and after they vet your organization they hand back a certificate — their signed statement that this public key belongs to you.

You then bundle key + certificate + IdenTrust's two CA certificates into one .p12 keystore for the MeF SDK, and upload the public certificate to the IRS's Automated Enrollment so MeF recognizes it at login.

Why a certificate at all — and why IdenTrust

The problem: MeF A2A is a machine calling a machine. There is no human to type a password, and a password on a server is just a string anyone who copies it can replay. The IRS's answer is mutual TLS with client certificates: your system proves its identity with a private key it never transmits, and the IRS checks the matching public certificate it has on file.

But the IRS won't take your word that a public key is yours — someone the IRS already trusts has to vouch for it. That someone is a Certificate Authority (CA), and for MeF A2A the approved CA program is IdenTrust's IGC — IdenTrust Global Common. IdenTrust vets your organization once, then signs your public key; the IRS trusts IdenTrust's signature, therefore it trusts your key.

Analogy — a notarized signature card

Your bank doesn't verify your handwriting itself — it keeps a signature card that a notary witnessed. The private key is your hand; the certificate is the notarized card; IdenTrust is the notary the IRS accepts. Anyone can claim a signature; only the card on file, vouched by the notary, makes the bank honor it.

Where this breaksA signature card is compared by eye and can be forged with practice. Certificate checks are mathematical: without the private key, no amount of copying the certificate (which is public) lets an attacker pass the check. That's why protecting the key file is the entire security model.

Day 0

Step 1 — Pick the right IGC product

Where: identrust.com → Certificates → IGC → IRS programs

IdenTrust sells several IGC certificate types and the wrong one wastes a purchase: certificates issued to a person (Individual/Affiliated) require that person present at use, which an unattended server can't do. A transmitter system needs a Device certificate — issued to a machine identity (an FQDN) under the organization's name.

  • On identrust.com, follow the “IRS – Modernized e-File (MeF)” path in their certificate selection wizard — it filters to the IGC products the IRS accepts.
  • Choose “IGC Standard Medium Assurance | Organization Identity | Device” — the product selected for this project. It's an organization-identity device certificate, which is why it's issued by IGC Device CA 2.
  • Storage form: choose software (downloadable, exportable to PKCS12) — not a hardware token/HSM option, which can't be loaded by the MeF SDK on a server.
  • Validity: buy the longest offered (typically 2 years). Every renewal repeats steps 3–8, so fewer renewals = fewer chances to break production. Check current pricing at purchase; budget a few hundred dollars per period.
i

Decide the FQDN now. The certificate's Common Name is fixed at issuance — this project used c2s.pahealthmanagement.org. Pick a hostname you control and won't rename within the cert's lifetime; a rename means a re-issue, not an edit.

Step 2 — Order, pay, and pass IdenTrust's vetting

Who: an authorized representative of the organization

IdenTrust's signature is only worth something because they check before they sign. The order form collects what they'll verify — fill it to match official records exactly, because mismatches are the #1 cause of week-long delays:

  • Organization legal name and address — exactly as registered (state records / D&B). This must also match your IRS e-file application; the IRS cross-references the certificate's O= field. Here: Pyramos Software.
  • The device FQDN from step 1 — IdenTrust verifies your organization controls the domain (DNS or email challenge to the domain's registered contacts).
  • The applicant — a real person authorized to request certificates for the org. Expect a verification callback to a publicly listed phone number, not the number you typed on the form.
  • Payment — card at order time.

Then you wait. Vetting runs a few days to two weeks. When it clears, IdenTrust emails retrieval instructions with an account/activation code — you need those codes plus the CSR from step 3 to collect the certificate.

Step 3 — Generate the private key and the CSR

Where: the server (or a secured admin machine) — never a shared laptop

Here's the elegant trick that makes the whole system safe: IdenTrust never needs your private key to certify it. A keypair has two halves — whatever one half encrypts, only the other can decrypt. So you keep one half (the private key) and send only the other half, wrapped in a CSR — Certificate Signing Request: a small signed file saying “here is my public key, and here is who I claim to be.”

# 1. Generate a 2048-bit RSA private key (IGC minimum; 3072/4096 also fine)
#    -nodes = no passphrase on the key file. See the note below before choosing.
openssl genrsa -out private-irs.key 2048
chmod 600 private-irs.key          # owner-read only — this file IS your identity

# 2. Create the CSR. Subject fields must match your IdenTrust order:
#    C  = country, O = exact legal org name, CN = the device FQDN from step 1
openssl req -new \
  -key private-irs.key \
  -out irs-mef.csr \
  -subj "/C=US/O=Pyramos Software/CN=c2s.pahealthmanagement.org"

# 3. Read back what you just built — verify before submitting
openssl req -in irs-mef.csr -noout -subject -text | head -20
Passphrase or not? (-nodes vs encrypted key)

An encrypted key file needs its passphrase typed (or supplied) every time it's loaded — awkward for an unattended server. The common server pattern is an unencrypted key protected by strict file permissions and disk encryption, then a password-protected PKCS12 built from it in step 6 (the SDK supplies that password from MEF_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD). If policy requires an encrypted raw key, add -aes256 to the genrsa command and be ready to enter its passphrase during step 6.

The browser-pickup alternative (and why CSR is better for servers)

IdenTrust's retrieval page can generate the keypair inside your browser instead of accepting a CSR. That marries the key to one person's browser profile — you then have to export it and move it to the server, an extra hop where the key can leak or get lost. The CSR route creates the key where it will live and it never moves. Use browser pickup only if the CSR option is unavailable for your product.

Key handling rules — the entire security model lives here

  • The private key never leaves the machine: not in email, not in Slack, not in the git repo. (.gitignore should cover *.key and *.p12.)
  • Keep one offline backup of the key in a password manager or sealed storage — lose the key and the paid certificate is scrap (see FAQ).
  • If the key is ever exposed, you revoke and re-issue (step 9); there is no “change the password” equivalent.

Step 4 — Submit the CSR and retrieve the issued certificate

Where: IdenTrust's retrieval portal, using the codes from step 2's email
  1. Open the retrieval link from IdenTrust's approval email and enter the account/activation codes.
  2. When prompted for the key, choose the CSR / “I will provide a PKCS#10” option and paste the full contents of irs-mef.csr (including the BEGIN/END CERTIFICATE REQUEST lines).
  3. IdenTrust signs it on the spot and returns your leaf certificate — download as PEM (Base64). If you're only offered DER (binary .cer), convert below.
# DER (binary) → PEM (Base64 text), if needed
openssl x509 -inform der -in downloaded.cer -out your-issued-cert.pem

# Inspect the issued certificate — check all four facts:
openssl x509 -in your-issued-cert.pem -noout -subject -issuer -dates -fingerprint
# subject = your org + FQDN        (C=US, O=Pyramos Software, CN=c2s.pahealthmanagement.org)
# issuer  = IGC Device CA 2        (the intermediate — NOT the root directly)
# dates   = today → +2 years       (calendar the notAfter date now, minus 60 days)

Also confirm the certificate really matches your key — the two must share the same public modulus:

# These two hashes must be identical, or the cert is for some other key
openssl x509 -in your-issued-cert.pem -noout -modulus | openssl md5
openssl rsa  -in private-irs.key       -noout -modulus | openssl md5

Step 5 — Download the CA chain and verify it end to end

Where: IdenTrust's certificate-chain download page

Your leaf alone can't be validated — the validator needs every link up to a trusted root. Download both CA certificates from IdenTrust (they publish them freely): the IGC Device CA 2 intermediate and the IdenTrust Global Common Root CA 1. In this repo they're saved as SubCA1.txt and Root.txt.

# Sanity-check each CA file
openssl x509 -in Root.txt   -noout -subject -issuer   # self-signed root, IGC Root CA 1
openssl x509 -in SubCA1.txt -noout -subject -issuer   # IGC Device CA 2, issued by the root

# The real test: does YOUR cert verify against this chain?
openssl verify -CAfile Root.txt -untrusted SubCA1.txt your-issued-cert.pem
# expected output:  your-issued-cert.pem: OK

If verify fails with “unable to get local issuer certificate”, your leaf was signed by a different intermediate (IdenTrust operates several — e.g. CA 2 vs CA 3). Read your leaf's issuer line and download exactly that intermediate.

Step 6 — Bundle key + chain into the PKCS12 keystore

Where: the server · Repo script: create-pkcs12.sh

The Java SDK doesn't read loose PEM files — it wants one password-protected container holding the private key and the full chain: a PKCS12 (.p12) keystore. Concatenate the chain leaf-first, then export:

# 1. Assemble the chain file — ORDER MATTERS: leaf, then intermediate, then root
cat your-issued-cert.pem SubCA1.txt Root.txt > irs-cert-chain.pem

# 2. Export to PKCS12 (this is what create-pkcs12.sh runs)
openssl pkcs12 -export \
  -in irs-cert-chain.pem \
  -inkey private-irs.key \
  -out irs-mef-test.p12 \
  -name "IRS MEF Test Certificate" \
  -certfile irs-cert-chain.pem
# prompts: (a) key passphrase, if you encrypted it in step 3
#          (b) a NEW export password for the .p12 — twice

Two values you choose here get wired into the app later, and they must match exactly:

-name (the alias)
IRS MEF Test Certificate → becomes MEF_KEY_ALIAS
export password
→ becomes MEF_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD (and MEF_KEY_PASSWORD — PKCS12 uses one password for both)
The trap
An alias typo doesn't fail loudly — the SDK finds no key under that alias and authentication just fails. Check the alias with step 7 before blaming the IRS.

Step 7 — Verify the keystore before touching the IRS

Repo script: verify-pkcs12.sh

Thirty seconds of verification here saves hours of debugging opaque login failures later. The keystore must contain exactly one private-key entry and three certificates — that's precisely what verify-pkcs12.sh asserts:

# Java's view — this is what the SDK will actually see
keytool -list -v -keystore irs-mef-test.p12 -storetype PKCS12
# Expect: "Your keystore contains 1 entry"
#         Alias name: irs mef test certificate   (keytool lowercases it)
#         Entry type: PrivateKeyEntry
#         Certificate chain length: 3
#           [1] CN=c2s.pahealthmanagement.org  (leaf)
#           [2] CN=IGC Device CA 2             (intermediate)
#           [3] CN=IdenTrust Global Common Root CA 1  (root)

# OpenSSL's view — cross-check
openssl pkcs12 -info -in irs-mef-test.p12 -nokeys

Chain length 1 instead of 3 means step 6's cat order or -certfile went wrong — rebuild; don't ship it, some validators reject an incomplete chain even when others tolerate it.

Step 8 — Register the public certificate with IRS Automated Enrollment

Who: Responsible Official · Where: e-Services → MeF Automated Enrollment (AE)

IdenTrust vouching for you isn't enough — MeF also keeps its own list of exactly which certificate each system (ASID) is allowed to log in with. Upload the public leaf only (never the .p12, never the key):

# AE upload forms typically want the binary DER form (.cer)
openssl x509 -in your-issued-cert.pem -outform der -out upload-to-irs.cer
  1. In the AE application, open your A2A system (ASID 23868900 for this project).
  2. Upload/replace the certificate on that system record.
  3. Confirm the fingerprint the portal shows matches your local one (openssl x509 -fingerprint from step 4) — proof you uploaded the right file.

From this point the login handshake works end to end: your client signs with the private key, MeF matches the presented certificate against the one registered on ASID 23868900, and the SAML session opens. Wire the keystore into the app (MEF_KEYSTORE_PATH, MEF_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD, MEF_KEY_ALIAS…) and run the repo's test-mef-login.sh against ATS to prove it.

every ~2 yrs

Step 9 — Renewal, replacement, revocation

Recurring — put notAfter minus 60 days in a shared calendar today

The certificate is the component of your MeF stack that will fail on a schedule. The failure mode is nasty: no code changed, no config changed, and one morning every login returns an authentication error. Prevent it with a 60-day-lead renewal ritual:

  1. ~60 days out: start a renewal order with IdenTrust (renewals of an unexpired cert usually skip full re-vetting — cheaper and faster than letting it lapse).
  2. Generate a fresh key + CSR (repeat step 3 — re-using the old key is allowed by some CAs but a new key is better hygiene) and retrieve the new leaf (step 4).
  3. Build a new .p12 (steps 5–7) alongside the old one — don't overwrite; you want instant rollback.
  4. Register the new cert in AE (step 8) before the old one expires, then switch MEF_KEYSTORE_PATH and restart. Verify with a login against ATS, then production.

When things go wrong

  • Key compromised or lost: request revocation through IdenTrust immediately, then re-issue from step 3. A revoked cert fails IRS validation within CRL/OCSP propagation time.
  • Renewed with IdenTrust but forgot AE: logins fail even though the cert is valid — MeF matches against its registered copy, not the CA's records. Step 8 is part of renewal, always.
  • Intermediate changed: IdenTrust rotates issuing CAs over the years (CA 2 today). At each renewal, re-read the new leaf's issuer and refresh SubCA1.txt if it changed.

FAQ

Individual, Affiliated, or Device certificate?
Device, for a transmitter server. Individual/Affiliated certs bind to a person and suit humans logging into IRS web portals. An unattended Spring Boot service authenticating A2A is a machine identity — exactly what this repo's cert is (issued by IGC Device CA 2).
Does the CSR expose anything sensitive?
No. It carries your public key and identity fields, signed by your private key to prove you hold it. It's safe to paste into IdenTrust's portal or email — though the private key file next to it never is.
Can the same certificate serve ATS and production?
Yes. The certificate authenticates who you are, not which environment you're calling; ATS vs PRD is just an endpoint choice (ats_endpoints.properties vs prd_endpoints.properties). One cert, registered once in AE, covers both.
What if we lose the private key but still have the .p12?
You're fine — the .p12 contains the private key; extract it with openssl pkcs12 -in irs-mef-test.p12 -nocerts -nodes -out recovered.key. Lost the .p12 and the raw key? The certificate is unusable; revoke it and re-issue from step 3.
Why three certificates in the keystore instead of just mine?
Some TLS stacks demand the client present its full chain so the server can validate without hunting for intermediates. Shipping leaf + intermediate + root in the .p12 costs nothing and removes a whole class of “works locally, fails at the IRS” bugs — it's why verify-pkcs12.sh asserts exactly 3.
RSA 2048 — is that enough?
It's the IGC baseline and what the MeF SDK expects; 3072/4096 also work if policy demands, at a negligible handshake cost. What you can't use: ECDSA keys or self-signed certs — the IRS trusts the IdenTrust chain, full stop.
